A new website rarely arrives as a neat list of pages. It arrives as a goal, a few examples, and a handful of things that already went wrong on the last site.

That pile is the brief. The useful first step is not a visual direction. It is deciding what a visitor has to understand, and what they have to do next, before anyone picks a typeface.

## Start with the job of the page

If the practice is new, the homepage has to introduce the doctors and make a consultation obvious. If the company sells technical equipment, the homepage has to get an engineer to the right product. Those are different sites, even when the tools are the same.

I write that job down before the layout. Pages, flows, and what has to stay consistent from one location or product to the next. Wireframes are just that plan made visible enough to argue with.

## Stay through the build

A handoff is where a clear idea gets sanded down. The layout that looked right in a frame turns out to be awkward to build, or the code starts drifting from the content that sold the design. When the same person is still in the work, that shows up early enough to fix.

The sites I want to write about here are the ones where that sorting actually mattered: healthcare practices that needed a patient path, and industrial companies whose buyers already knew what they were looking for.
